About these layers

Elevation: AWS Terrain Tiles (a public blend of SRTM, USGS 3DEP and GMTED), fetched at the zoom that matches the map and resampled onto the map’s own latitude/longitude grid — never a stretched web-Mercator image. Contours are round numbers in your height unit; every fifth is heavier and labelled. Sun exposure is the direct sunlight a slope receives divided by what flat ground at the same place receives: gold = more sun than flat ground (a south-facing slope in the northern winter), indigo = less; the colour is full strength at 1.41× and 0.71× (half a stop), which spans most real slopes. “Over today” sums the whole day’s Sun track at the map centre; “At this time” uses the Sun at each point at the time on the timeline (night is blank). It is clear-sky geometry only: no clouds, no haze, and no shadows cast by neighbouring ridges — a slope only shades itself. Orographic lift is the vertical wind the slope forces on the 10 m wind at the surface, w = u·∂h/∂x + v·∂h/∂y: red where the wind blows up the slope (forced ascent), blue where it blows down it. It is the surface value only — above the ground it fades over a height about the size of the hill, and a stable atmosphere turns it into lee waves this does not model. The wind is GFS’s, on GFS’s own ~25 km terrain, so a slope can feel a different wind; and w is only as fine as the slope grid. A diagnostic of where the flow is pushed, not a promise of lift at a spot. Slope winds are Prandtl’s (1942) steady flow along each slope’s fall line: air warmed by a sunlit slope slides up it (anabatic, amber), air chilled by a slope losing heat at night drains down it (katabatic, blue). Each slope’s heating is its own: the Sun at the time on the timeline on that slope’s face, minus the night-sky heat loss, both reduced by GFS cloud cover. The speed is an order of magnitude — the heat-transfer coefficient, background stability and mixing are fixed typical values, not measured here — while the direction and the timing (an east face turns upslope first) are the layer’s real claims. Gentle slopes are left blank: the flow takes longer to set up than the heating lasts. Not modelled: shadows cast by other ridges, the valley wind and the large-scale wind on top, and the turbulence of a hot afternoon.

About this layer

Thermals are columns of air warmed by the ground that rise through the daytime boundary layer. Their strength is the convective velocity w* (Deardorff 1970) from our GFS archive’s surface heat flux and boundary-layer height; they stop at the top of that layer, or lower at the cloud base, where the rising air saturates and builds cumulus — computed exactly (Romps 2017) from the 2 m temperature and dew point. Without cumulus they are “blue” thermals, unmarked in the sky. The heat flux is GFS’s mean over the 3-hour window containing the time on the timeline. This is a 25 km field: the sunny slope or dry field that actually releases a thermal is not resolved, and w* is the size of the updraughts, not a climb rate — a glider climbs at roughly w* minus its own sink. Weak / moderate / strong follow the soaring-forecast convention (under 1, 1–2, over 2 m/s). Heights are above the ground.

Updraft is GFS’s own vertical velocity at a pressure level — 850 hPa (~1.5 km up at sea level), 700 hPa (~3 km), 500 hPa (~5.5 km) — averaged over its 25 km grid cell: the rising and sinking of fronts, troughs, mountain waves and big storms. Where a level is below the ground (850 hPa over the Rockies) it is left blank — the model’s value there is extrapolated, not air. Each level’s height above the ground is estimated from the surface pressure and temperature. The side panel lists every vertical motion the map knows at the site — the slope’s push at the ground, the slope wind, the thermals’ strength, and these grid means — side by side and never added: they are different things at different heights.

Storm ingredients checks the three things a thunderstorm needs. Energy: CAPE, the energy a rising surface parcel would gain from being warmer than the air around it (GFS’s own surface-based value; under 100 J/kg there is nothing to release). The lid: CIN, the energy needed to push the parcel up to where it starts to rise on its own — a warm layer aloft that holds storms down. Lift: the thermals from the layer above. “Capped” means energy under a lid the lift cannot push through (½·w² < |CIN|); “possible”, a weak lid (|CIN| ≤ 25 J/kg) but no strong thermals; “likely”, a weak lid with thermals of at least 1 m/s. The lifted index is the parcel’s temperature excess at 500 hPa, with the sign flipped: negative is unstable. GFS’s CIN is itself a forecast that weakens through the afternoon as the ground warms the air, so a lid “breaking” here is the model’s own heating. This is an ingredients check on a 25 km grid, not a probability or a warning: forced lift over terrain, fronts and storm outflows are not counted, and moisture is only what is inside CAPE. √(2·CAPE) is the speed a parcel would reach with no mixing — real updraughts reach about half. The same CAPE, CIN and lifted index can be charted for the site under Explore.

What you are looking at

Reanalysis, not a station. The long record is ERA5 / ERA5-Land: a physics model fitted to every observation available, on a ~9–25 km grid. It is the best continuous record that exists for most places on Earth, but it is a grid cell, not your thermometer — the catalogue prints how far away the grid point is.

Forecast, labelled as one. The hourly series are the Home Station's numerical-weather-prediction feed. Everything to the right of "now" is drawn dashed and shaded: a projection, not an observation. The hours just before now are the model's own analysis, not a measurement.

Long windows are aggregated, never thinned. On a decade or more each series is reduced into fixed-length buckets by its own rule — a daily high keeps the bucket's maximum, rain is summed, a temperature is averaged — and the legend names the bucket, so a 30-day maximum is never read as a daily line. A bucket with too few samples is drawn as a gap, not a zero.

Where the data comes from today. Every source is public. The long archive currently comes through a public reanalysis API used as a bootstrap; Climalytix's own ingest of the underlying public archives replaces it. The forecast ladder, the event builder and scored predictions come next — every probability Climalytix issues will carry its own verification record at your site.
