Chicxulub, backwards

Sixty-six million years ago something hit the Yucatán shelf and ended the Cretaceous. Nobody saw it. What exists is a buried crater, a thin layer of iridium around the whole planet, and beds of debris that thin with distance. This page starts from those measurements and works backwards — to the size and speed of the body, the direction it came from and the orbit it was on — and then forwards again to what it did.

Tiles show the reconstruction's median and 68 % range for a main-belt asteroid. It is a scaling-law reconstruction, not a shock-physics simulation; the scaling coefficients and some evidence values were written from memory of the literature and are marked as unchecked below.

The clock is linear for the approach and stretches after impact, so seconds, hours and decades each get room; the strip above the slider shows sunlight (white) and global temperature (blue) on that same stretched axis. The four rings on the planet are how far the shaking, the falling debris, the air blast and the tsunami have reached, at the speeds used in Step 3 — they stop being drawn at the far side of the planet. The darkness lasts as long as the published climate model found, and the temperature is the simple model from Step 4: it gets the depth of the cold right by construction and its recovery is too slow, so the clock stops at 30 years. The planet darkens with the sky but is never drawn black.

What is real here: the body's size, speed, angle and bearing, the two crater sizes and depths, the time it takes to cross the air and to bury itself, where thrown-out rock is landing, and the peak ring's measured radius. What is a sketch: how the hole gets from one shape to the next — the growth of the bowl, the rebound of its floor, the height of the debris curtain, and the fireball's growth and fading are an interpolation timed to the published picture, not a shock-physics simulation. The asteroid's lumpy shape is invented; nothing is known about it. The ground is drawn plain because the coastline of 66 million years ago is not in this model, and the sea, the layered rock and the returning water are not drawn.

      What this is, and what it is not

      Crater scaling. Impactor → transient crater → final rim uses the Collins, Melosh & Marcus (2005) scaling laws, with no shock physics, no layered target and no sea. The laws predict the topographic rim, taken here as the 140–170 km inner rim; reading the 180–200 km outer ring as the rim instead makes the body about a fifth larger and nearly doubles the energy. That choice is the largest uncertainty on this page.

      Iridium. The layer's 55 ng/cm² over the whole Earth is about 280,000 tonnes of iridium. In a CM-type body that is the iridium of a ~7 km asteroid. A 7 km body would need to arrive faster than anything bound to the Sun can to dig this crater, and the ruthenium isotopes say it was an asteroid, not a comet. So the reconstruction lets the layer hold only a fraction of the impactor's iridium and reports that fraction. Where the rest went — the crater, the nearby ejecta, or off the planet — is not modelled.

      Trajectory. Exact two-body mechanics from the ground out of Earth's gravity, then Earth's orbital velocity added. The crater is placed where a published plate model puts it 66 million years ago (Müller et al. 2019: 21.9°N instead of today's 21.3°N, with its compass turned about 12° clockwise, so "from the north-east" in today's geography is turned the same way). That model's frame is fixed to the mantle, not to the spin axis, so a few degrees of polar wander are not in it. It also uses today's axial tilt and a circular orbit for Earth; neither is known for 66 million years ago. "From the north-east" is read as a bearing of 45° ± 30°.

      Climate. One box with one heat capacity. The length of the darkness is taken from the published climate-model runs, not predicted; the box predicts only the temperature, and with a shallow ocean layer it over-cools about fourfold. It is here to show what the published answer implies, not to replace it.
