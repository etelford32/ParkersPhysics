How deep are the ocean's trenches, and how cold is the water at the bottom? Depth here comes from a global bathymetry grid; temperature comes from the deepest real casts ever archived near each site in NOAA's World Ocean Database. Where no instrument reached the floor, the profile is continued by physics and marked as modelled. One result surprises most people: below about four kilometres the water gets warmer with depth, because it is squeezed. Turn the globe, click anywhere on the ocean and dive under the surface to the sea floor. Then fire the echo sounder to see why measuring depth is harder than it sounds.

Where every number comes from

Depth: a grid, not a survey. The sea floor is the ocean part of the public AWS Terrain Tiles: NOAA ETOPO1 (one arc-minute) with GEBCO 2014. Most of the deep ocean in it is depth predicted from satellite gravity and tied to sparse ship tracks, on cells about 1.8 km across. A trench's deepest grid cell therefore differs from the best ship or lander sounding by tens to a few hundred metres. The table shows the grid's value and, beside it, the commonly quoted figure. Those quoted figures were written from memory and have not been checked against their sources. The bake date is —.

Temperature: measured where an instrument went. Casts are from the NOAA NCEI World Ocean Database (CTD rosettes, bottle casts and, from 2014, profiling floats), read from NOAA's public archive. For each site the eight deepest casts within 150 km are kept. Levels that the database's own quality control flagged are dropped, not repaired. Profiles are thinned for download size, never averaged. A cast is a snapshot on its own date; the deep sea changes slowly, but it does change.

Below the deepest level: modelled. Where the cast stops short of the floor, the last measured water is carried down adiabatically (no heat exchanged, salinity held), which is how well-mixed trench water behaves. This uses the UNESCO 1983 seawater algorithms (Bryden's adiabatic gradient). It ignores geothermal heating and is wrong if different water lies below. Where the deepest cast extends far enough, the page tests the model on it by hiding its lower part and predicting it, and prints the error.

Sound speed and soundings. Sound speed is Chen & Millero (UNESCO 1983) from temperature, salinity and pressure. It differs from the newer TEOS-10 standard by up to about 2 m/s at trench pressure, which is roughly 15 m of echo depth at 11 km, and soundings here carry that uncertainty. The echo is timed straight down; the beam test uses straight rays on the 1.8 km grid, so a real narrow beam would resolve walls this grid cannot. Depth from pressure is the UNESCO standard-ocean formula.

Diving anywhere. The 28 marked sites are stored with the page. Anywhere else, the sea floor is read at the moment you dive from the same public tiles, at the same 1.8 km detail. Temperature is shown only where an archived cast lies within 150 km of your point; elsewhere the page shows pressure and light but no temperature.

Light under water. Sunlight fades by about 4 % per metre in the clearest open ocean, so about 1 % is left at 115 m and effectively none below a few hundred metres. That rate is an order-of-magnitude figure written from memory, not checked against its source. In the dive view the water is drawn clear and the sea floor is lit artificially, because real sea water lets you see only tens of metres; only the background colour follows the light at your depth.

Not to scale: the relief on the globe and the vertical scale of the sea-floor block, both printed on the view and adjustable down to 1×. The ping's flight is shown on a compressed clock; the real time is printed.
