What this is, and what it is not

The model. Müller, R. D., et al. (2019), A global plate model including lithospheric deformation along major rifts and orogens since the Triassic, Tectonics 38 — the EarthByte group's model, version 2.0, from their own server, used under CC BY-SA 4.0. The plate outlines and rotations on this page are derived from it and are shared under the same licence. Each plate's motion is a chain of rotations about poles, interpolated between the model's dated rows the same way the model's own software does it.

Checked against the reference software. The same model files were run through pyGPlates, the EarthByte group's own library, and this page's code is tested against it:

    The outlines are today's. Every shape on the globe is a piece of the present-day surface carried back on its plate. The tan areas are the model's outlines of continental crust, not past coastlines, and the light lines are today's coastlines. Shallow seas have flooded and drained the continents many times; that history is not in this model. The dark areas are ocean floor that existed then but has since sunk back into the mantle, so nothing of it is left to carry back.

    The plates move as rigid pieces. The model also describes how rifts and mountain belts stretch and squeeze, but this page moves each piece rigidly. Expect small gaps and overlaps where the crust has deformed, such as the Andes, Tibet and the North Sea.

    Two models, two frames. The globe and the first answer are Müller et al. (2019), which fixes its reference to the deep mantle. The second model, in pink, is Merdith, A. S., et al. (2021), Extending full-plate tectonic models into deep time, Earth-Science Reviews 214 (version 1.2.4, from the same EarthByte server, under CC BY 4.0). It fixes its reference to the spin axis as ancient magnetised rocks record it. Over tens of millions of years the whole solid Earth also tilts relative to the spin axis (true polar wander), so the two frames give different latitudes even where the plates move the same way.

    What their difference means. For the last 250 million years Merdith's model is compiled from an earlier EarthByte model of the same family as Müller's, so the two are not independent. Where they disagree, the truth is at least that uncertain. Where they agree, it may still be wrong in the same way in both. Palaeomagnetism measures latitude but not longitude, so the latitude difference is the meaningful one. The distance apart also includes a longitude that each frame fixes by its own convention.

    Seafloor age comes from the age at which each strip of ocean floor first appears in the model (its isochron bins), so it is the age of that strip to within its bin, not a measured age grid. The geological periods on the slider are from the International Chronostratigraphic Chart, written from memory; they label the slider and are not used in any calculation. The model ends at 250 million years and the page never goes past it.
