Plates Through Time

1.8 billion years · reconstructed · measured

Earth's tectonic plates from 1.8 billion years ago to today, moved by a published full-plate reconstruction (Cao et al., extending Merdith et al. 2021). Continents travel exactly as the model's rotations carry them; ridges and trenches are its plate boundaries. Every number beside them — boundary lengths, sea floor made and destroyed, plate speeds, how tightly the continents cluster — is measured here, frame by frame, and checked against the model authors' own published figures.

What this is — and is not

  • A kinematic reconstruction, not a simulation of the mantle. The model's authors fitted the motions to palaeomagnetic poles, geology and, since about 200 Ma, the magnetic stripes of the ocean floor. Nothing here solves for the forces that drive plates.
  • Before ~200 Ma the oceans are inferred. No sea floor older than that survives, so oceanic plates, ridges and trenches in deep time are drawn to fit the continents' motion. The model's own notes say the great external ocean (Panthalassa) is poorly constrained.
  • Latitude is measured; longitude mostly is not. Palaeomagnetism fixes how far a continent was from the pole, not where it sat round the globe. The map's frame is the model's palaeomagnetic frame.
  • The light land is today's coastline, carried back on its plate — a way to recognise each piece, not the ancient shoreline (sea level and the land itself were different). The darker ground is continental crust including shelves, as the model outlines it.
  • Continents move continuously; boundaries are resolved every 5 Myr. Between those frames each boundary piece is carried by its own plate's rotation. Where the model changes a boundary's shape, it changes at a frame.
  • Some instants are the model's own defects, shown as such. Near 855 Ma its plate polygons fail to close (hatched, "no plate"). Where a plate's rotation jumps (for example at 1000 Ma, where two rotation files meet), positions jump as the model has them and velocities are taken from the continuous side.
  • "Supercontinent index" is our measure: how tightly continental area clusters (0 = spread round the globe, 1 = one mass). It knows no names or dates; its peaks land on Pangaea, Gondwana, Rodinia and Nuna by themselves. Only crust the model includes at each time counts, so deep-time values rest on less crust.

Before 1.8 billion years

The scrubber stops at 1.8 Ga because no plate reconstruction reaches further: older crust is too scattered, too deformed and too sparsely dated for its motions to be fitted. Earth is 4.57 billion years old, and when plate tectonics began — more than 3 billion years ago, or not until about 2 billion — is still argued over. Rather than draw maps nobody can check, this page stops where the evidence does.
